# Section 1: Introduction — The Premature Critique

## Draft v0.1 — for review and iteration

---

The governance of artificial intelligence has become one of the defining intellectual and political preoccupations of the present decade. Regulatory frameworks have been enacted or proposed across major jurisdictions. Academic output on algorithmic accountability, fairness-aware machine learning, and AI ethics has grown at a rate that outpaces any individual scholar's capacity to survey it. Civil society organizations, parliamentary committees, and international bodies have converged on a shared set of concerns: that AI systems exhibit discriminatory behavior, that their decision processes are opaque, that their outputs are difficult to contest, and that the concentration of their development in a small number of powerful institutions raises fundamental questions about democratic governance.

These concerns are legitimate. The work being done to address them is serious. This paper argues, nonetheless, that the overwhelming majority of it is premature.

Not premature in the sense of being ahead of its time. Premature in the precise sense that a clinical intervention is premature when it addresses a presenting symptom while leaving the underlying pathology unexamined. The current discourse on AI governance is focused, almost without exception, on what AI systems do — on their outputs, their behavior, their impacts on the populations they affect. It has systematically failed to examine the layer that determines what AI systems can do: the constitutive conditions of the data assets from which their inferential capacity is built.

This failure is not incidental. It reflects a shared and largely unexamined assumption that runs through data protection law, algorithmic accountability frameworks, open data initiatives, and fairness-aware machine learning alike: that data, once collected, is a pre-ethical given whose governance problems emerge downstream of its production. On this assumption, ethical work begins after the data exists. The present paper argues that this assumption is the foundational error of contemporary data governance — and that correcting it requires both a different philosophical framework and a different class of procedural instrument than anything currently available.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 establishes the constitutive problem in data: the systematic encoding of interests, exclusions, and assumptions into data assets prior to any downstream use, through mechanisms — scope capture, publication bias, exclusion by design — that are structurally incentivized and currently ungoverned. Section 3 demonstrates that existing governance frameworks share a common architectural failure: each addresses genuine concerns downstream of data constitution while leaving the constitutive layer untouched, and in doing so generates false confidence that adequate epistemic scrutiny has been applied when the most fundamental layer of that scrutiny is absent. Section 4 introduces Rational, Empathy-Informed Ethics (REE) as the philosophical framework adequate to the constitutive problem — grounded in the foundational question of whose experiences populate the nodes from which inferential capacity is built, and organized around five principles that are both philosophically coherent and operationalizable in practice. Section 5 presents the OpenReason Protocol (ORP) as the concrete operationalization of REE's philosophical architecture, demonstrating its operation through the case of the 2024 Danish property tax reform. Section 6 examines the implications of the argument for AI development, scientific publishing, and data-driven governance more broadly, acknowledges the limits of the present proposal, and identifies the open questions that define the research agenda the argument generates.

The claim of this paper is not that model-level accountability is unimportant. It is that model-level accountability is insufficient — and that the insufficiency is not a gap that can be closed by extending existing frameworks incrementally. It requires a foundational intervention at the layer where the problem originates: the constitutive conditions of data assets themselves. REE provides the philosophical grounding for that intervention. ORP provides its procedural form. Together, they constitute not a supplement to existing data governance frameworks but the missing foundation on which adequate governance must rest.
