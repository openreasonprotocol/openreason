# Section 6: Implications, Limits, and Open Questions

## Draft v0.1 — for review and iteration

---

The argument presented in this paper has three components whose implications extend beyond the specific governance proposal they support. The constitutive problem is a diagnosis of a structural condition in data-driven reasoning that affects domains far beyond those examined here. REE is a philosophical framework whose application to data governance is one instance of a broader project. ORP is a working protocol at an early stage of development whose current form is the beginning of a research programme rather than its conclusion. This section addresses each in turn, before identifying the limits of the present argument and the open questions they generate.

### 6.1 Implications for Data-Driven Governance

The most immediate implication of the constitutive problem diagnosis is a requirement for epistemic humility about the reliability of existing data-driven governance decisions. If the constitutive conditions of data assets are systematically undocumented, and if those conditions encode interests, exclusions, and assumptions in ways that downstream governance frameworks cannot detect, then the body of decisions that have been made on the basis of such assets — in policy, in science, in institutional knowledge — carries an unknown but non-trivial error rate whose sources cannot be retrospectively traced.

This is not a counsel of paralysis. Data-driven governance, even under conditions of constitutive distortion, may produce better decisions than the alternatives available. The implication is not that data-driven decisions should be abandoned but that their epistemic status should be represented accurately — as conclusions reached under documented conditions of uncertainty, rather than as findings delivered by neutral processes of analysis. The difference between these representations is not merely rhetorical; it determines whether the self-correction mechanisms of rational inquiry can operate on the conclusions reached.

For artificial intelligence development specifically, the implications are substantial. The present discourse on AI safety, alignment, and accountability is overwhelmingly focused on model behavior — on what systems do given their training. The constitutive problem establishes that this focus is incomplete in a specific and consequential way: model behavior is a function of training data, and training data is a function of constitutive decisions whose ethical character is currently ungoverned. Efforts to align model outputs with human values that do not address the values encoded in training data constitution are efforts to correct at the wrong layer. The present paper does not argue that model-level accountability is unimportant; it argues that it is insufficient without the constitutive-layer accountability that REE grounds and ORP implements.

For scientific publishing, the implications reinforce and extend the replication crisis literature. The file drawer problem and publication bias are well-documented; what the constitutive problem adds is an account of the institutional mechanism that produces them — the structural incentive misalignment between private funding interests and public epistemic goods — and a framework for what adequate transparency about that mechanism would require. ORP's Layer 1, applied to scientific datasets, would require disclosure of funding relationships and scope decisions that current publishing norms do not mandate. This is a significant extension of open science principles from data access to data constitution.

### 6.2 Implications for REE as a Philosophical Framework

This paper has applied REE to a specific domain — the constitutive conditions of data assets in governance and research contexts. The framework's architecture suggests broader applicability that the present paper cannot fully develop but that warrants identification.

REE's foundational reorientation — from evaluating what reasoning systems do given their inputs, to examining whose experiences populated the nodes from which their reasoning proceeds — applies wherever inferential capacity is built from accumulated experience. This includes, but is not limited to, artificial intelligence training. It includes the formation of institutional knowledge through organizational practice. It includes the development of professional expertise through training and credentialing processes that determine whose cases are treated as canonical. In each of these domains, the constitutive question — whose experiences were included, under what conditions, by whose decisions, in whose interests — is both ethically significant and currently underaddressed.

REE does not claim to resolve all ethical questions in these domains. Its contribution is more specific: it provides a framework for identifying where ethical responsibility is located — at the constitutive layer — and for generating the procedural requirements that follow from that location. The framework's operationalizability, argued in Section 4.4, means that these requirements can in principle be implemented without waiting for broader normative consensus. ORP demonstrates this in one domain; the framework's architecture suggests it could be instantiated in others.

### 6.3 Limits of the Present Argument

Intellectual honesty requires explicit acknowledgment of what the present argument does not establish and what objections it does not fully resolve.

**The verification problem.** ORP requires documentation of constitutive decisions — scope choices, exclusion criteria, funding relationships, methodological alternatives. But documentation can be incomplete, selectively curated, or produced in forms that satisfy formal requirements while concealing substantive distortions. A protocol that requires transparency can be gamed by agents who understand its requirements well enough to comply with their letter while violating their spirit. The present paper does not resolve this problem; it establishes the requirement for transparency as a necessary condition for epistemic accountability without claiming that documented transparency is a sufficient condition. The development of verification mechanisms — independent audit processes, algorithmic consistency checking, community scrutiny through the fork registry — is a research agenda that ORP's architecture enables but does not complete.

**The adoption problem.** A protocol that is philosophically well-grounded and technically implemented is not thereby adopted. The incentive structures that produce constitutive distortion — the same structures identified in Section 2 — also operate against the adoption of transparency requirements that would make those distortions visible. Pharmaceutical companies have no immediate incentive to implement ORP's funding disclosure requirements. Governments have no immediate incentive to document the scope decisions that shaped the administrative datasets their policies rest on. The conditions under which adoption becomes rational — regulatory mandate, reputational incentive, civil society pressure — are not examined in the present paper and represent a significant open question for the governance of ORP's own diffusion.

**The scope limitation.** The present paper focuses on the constitutive conditions of data assets in policy reasoning and AI development. It does not examine the constitutive problem in oral knowledge traditions, in embodied expertise, or in forms of knowing that do not pass through documentable data production processes. REE's Universal Sentience principle implies that these domains are also subject to constitutive-layer ethical requirements, but the procedural implications of that implication in non-data contexts require development that the present paper does not undertake.

**The recursive problem.** ORP documents are themselves data assets, produced under conditions that carry their own constitutive character. The framework that requires transparency about data production is not exempt from the requirement for transparency about its own production. This recursive challenge does not invalidate the framework — all governance frameworks face analogous recursion problems — but it warrants acknowledgment. ORP's governance documentation and its fork registry are partial responses to this challenge; a fuller response requires the kind of ongoing community scrutiny that the protocol's architecture is designed to enable but cannot guarantee.

### 6.4 Open Questions and Research Agenda

The limits identified above generate a research agenda whose scope exceeds the present paper but whose structure it defines. Four questions are of particular priority.

**Verification mechanisms for constitutive transparency.** What audit processes, technical consistency checks, and community scrutiny mechanisms are adequate to the verification problem? This question sits at the intersection of epistemology, governance design, and technical implementation, and requires interdisciplinary engagement that the present paper cannot provide unilaterally.

**Adoption conditions and diffusion dynamics.** Under what conditions do institutions adopt constitutive-layer transparency requirements, and what governance instruments are effective in creating those conditions? This is partly an empirical question about institutional behavior and partly a normative question about the appropriate role of regulatory, market, and civil society mechanisms in data governance reform.

**Cross-domain instantiation of ORP.** How should ORP's layer architecture be adapted for application to AI training data constitution, scientific dataset production, and institutional knowledge formation? Each domain presents specific constitutive challenges that may require layer-level modifications while preserving the REE philosophical foundation. The development of domain-specific ORP profiles is a concrete research and implementation agenda.

**REE in non-data epistemic contexts.** How do REE's constitutive-layer requirements apply to forms of knowledge production that do not pass through documentable data assets? The extension of the framework to oral traditions, embodied expertise, and experiential knowledge raises questions about the relationship between REE's operationalizability requirement and forms of knowing that resist procedural documentation.

These questions are not peripheral to the argument of the present paper. They are its natural continuation — the open frontier that an honest account of the constitutive problem, adequately grounded in REE and demonstrated through ORP, necessarily produces. They define a research programme rather than a conclusion, which is the appropriate epistemic posture for a framework that holds its own claims, as REE requires, as hypotheses subject to revision rather than dogmas immune to challenge.
